# csc-115_final capstone project report
Purpose:
The purpose of this project was to create a model capable of predicting whether or not a patient was likely to readmitted to a hospital after release, based on a provided dataset. The dataset included several metrics on which to train models, twelve in total, varying in scale and type.

Dataset information:
Two features of the dataset were categorical, the remaining ten numeric. The categorical features were the biological sex of a patient and the nature of their admission (elective, urgent, or emergency). The numeric features included standard statistics such as age or BMI as well as less standard ones such as their number of chronic conditions and the number of prior admissions. The data also included information on the loads on the hospital and physicians, the severity of several aspects of their treatment and/or vitals, as mentioned above on different scales (1-100, 0-1).

Dataset issues:
Both of the categorical features were potentially significant. However, they were in a format that most models would not be able to use. Thus, the categorical features required transformation into a more useful form. In addition, every feature contained significant numbers of null data points. While not an overwhelming amount of data was missing, some kind of imputation would be required to render the data more usable. Finally, the varying values of the numeric data required scaling, as some were very small (e. g. physician workload, 0-1) compared to others which ranged higher (severity score, 0-100) or were statistical values about a patient, such as age.

Treatment of categorical features:
Of the two categorical features, biological sex was suitable for one-hot encoding, since there were only two values present, or three if counting null. The pandas library included a method for one-hot encoding, get_dummies, so this was accomplished fairly simply.
The admission type feature requires more specific handling. While sklearn.preprocessing had other means of encoding, neither of those available proved suitable, because neither LabelEncoder nor OrdinalEncoder was found to preserve the specific order of severity of the type of admission, or if they could the particulars on accomplishing this were out of reach. However, after some research1 a method for doing so manually was found. The initial numeric values set for each admission type were set to 1 for elective, 3 for urgent, and 5 for emergency. However, later in the process experimentation led to the best overall combination for the models being 1, 5, and 10, respectively.

Imputation method:
On the first few trials of the first three models, SimpleImputer was used. However, later in the process IterativeImputer and KNNImputer were experimented with, both in using each and in their parameters. KNNImputer ultimately gave the best results for all but one of the models used.

Modeling approach:
The initial model selected for a baseline was logistic regression. KNN was also considered, but with the number of features present the less computationally expensive model seemed a better choice for a first attempt. A decision tree was a third contender for first model, but its propensity for overfitting led to it not being chosen either. Another factor that led to the choice of logistic regression was the fairly even split in the target between readmission or not; the dataset was not imbalanced in this regard.
The first trial of logistic regression gave approximately .79 accuracy and roughly similar precision and recall, and refinement in the imputation method, scaling, and specific values for admission type gave marginal improvements. 
The second model chosen for trial was an SVC model, due to the somewhat good performance of logistic regression. However, it performed surprisingly poorly on both the first attempt and those after, in the range of .75 for accuracy, precision closer to that of logistic regression, and recall at .73.
A random forest model was the third candidate. It also showed an initial poor performance, overall similar to that of the SVC model. However, experimentation with the imputer used improved its metrics to the point that the reached in the final product.
The last model tried was KNN. Using this model was not part of the initial plan, but after the small improvements to every model given by eventually switching to KNNImputer the model itself seemed worth exploration. Unfortunately the KNN model performed overall poorly, similar in most respects to the SVC model. Perhaps noteworthy was that the KNN model performed worst with KNNImputer, and slightly better with SimpleImputer or IterativeImputer. Eventually the KNN model was given a specific pipeline that included SimpleImputer, but even so its metrics never substantially improved.

Results and interpretation:
Overall the model with the strongest performance was the first one used, the logistic regression. It had the greatest accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score, and thus the best combination of scores as well. False negatives are a particularly important measure in medical applications, and the logistic regression model’s performance on recall places it far ahead of the other models, even the second best, the random forest model. The accuracy (.84) does not suggest overfitting, especially with balanced classes. Even with the logistic regression being the best model, it is hard to recommend in its present state. The hope was to reach metrics better than those achieved by the end of this project, in particular with respect to recall.
The initial expectation was that the random forest model would perform best, given the nature of the classification problem. Its scores almost bear this out, but the high number of false negatives is troubling. Neither the KNN nor the SVC models performed well in any metric. The KNN model was put together out of curiosity, so its poorer scoring is not as surprising as the SVC model’s low outcomes.

Final Conclusions:
While none of the models had sub-.75 scores in their final form, none had any greater than .85. In the medical field it feels as though this is insufficient to recommend any of the models in their present form. More time spent with the best performers, logistic regression and random forest, might be able to push their accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 scores to the point where they could be considered usable, if not reliable, predictors.
